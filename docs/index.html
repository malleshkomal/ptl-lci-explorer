How 1 kg of fuel is made

Follow the mass. CO₂ from the air and hydrogen from water are turned into carbon monoxide, mixed into syngas, and built into fuel molecules. Ribbon width is kilograms per kg of finished fuel. Most of the mass that goes in leaves again as water.

    Water is not listed in the inventory. It is the difference between what goes into a step and what comes out, which matches the chemistry: CO₂ + H₂ → CO + H₂O, then CO + 2 H₂ → –CH₂– + H₂O.

    Where the electricity goes

    Wind electricity is the main input to both products. Electrolysis, which splits water into hydrogen and oxygen, uses almost all of it.

    How much of the energy ends up in the product

    Energy content of the product (lower heating value) compared with the electricity used. The rest is lost as heat along the way.

    Assumed heating values: hydrogen 33.3 kWh/kg (120 MJ/kg), kerosene-like fuel 11.9 kWh/kg (43 MJ/kg). These are standard values, not part of the dataset.

    Hydrogen and carbon

    Hydrogen is why the electricity bill is so high. The carbon side closes a loop: the CO₂ captured from the air roughly equals what the fuel releases when it is burned.

    Materials from the wider economy

    Besides electricity and water, each kg of product draws small amounts of materials, mostly steel for plant and equipment. These are the inputs that link the dataset to ecoinvent.

    Top material inputs, grams per kg of product

    Materials measured in kg only. Deionised water is left out because it is far larger (shown in the headline figures).

    Equipment lasts a long time

    Each plant or machine is shared by everything it produces in its lifetime. The dataset records how much of a unit one kg of output uses. The inverse is the total output over a unit's life.

    Inside the dataset

    The inventory is a list of activities. Each one lists its exchanges: what it takes from other activities in the dataset, from the ecoinvent background database, and from or to the environment.
